How to consciously choose the field you grow in

Ten exercises on the component that is "contagious", and on putting that knowledge to work for you

Imagine the same person in two different teams. In one, they're quiet, unnoticed, they don't voice their ideas and leave in the evening feeling empty. In the other, after a month you wouldn't recognise them: laughing, suggesting, arguing. The same person. The field changed, and it drew out what the first one was muffling.

Psychology has a name for this: emotional contagion. We pick up the states of the people around us without effort and without permission: someone else's calm calms us, someone else's anxiety winds up the anxiety in us, someone else's movement makes us want to move. Part of the explanation is linked to the so-called mirror neurons, cells that respond when you simply watch another person act. How much they explain in humans is still being studied. But the effect itself is well documented. It isn't a metaphor.

E has two layers. c, context: what was given without your participation, your early years, your family, your era. That really is hard to change. And ch, choice of environment: what you choose now, the people, the city, the voices in your ears, your professional circle. That's what this workbook works with.

Not "cut out the bad people". Become aware of the field you're standing in, and make conscious choices.

The main caveat: this workbook is not about "toxic people", and not about removing anyone from your life. Often the most important thing is simply to see what field surrounds you right now. Then come small, conscious choices. Even where there isn't complete freedom.

How it works

How to use this workbook

1
No judgement of people. The exercises are about observing the field, not passing verdicts on people. If someone "drains your energy", it's not because they're a bad person, but because that's how the relationship is right now.
2
Step by step, without rushing. First see the field, then check its influence, then make choices. Not the other way round.
3
Small choices are choices too. You can't always change your surroundings completely. But you can always add something or reduce something a little. Even that changes the field.
4
Tick an exercise when it has become yours. Not when you've read it, but when you've actually done it. The progress bar at the top shows the path you've walked.
Level 1 · Foundation
See your field

Our surroundings influence us all the time, but usually this stays outside our attention. The first step is simply to see this field as it is right now.

Exercise 1
A map of people's energy
mechanism: awareness of the emotional influence of your surroundings pain: "lonely among people, nobody understands"

This isn't about "good" and "bad" people. It's about how you come out of an interaction: fuller or emptier. Both states are information. They help you make more conscious choices about who you spend time with, and how.

How to do it
  1. Write down 8–10 people you talk to most often. Not the "closest", the most frequent, by actual time spent.
  2. Next to each, ask yourself: "how do I usually feel after talking to this person, fuller or emptier?" Mark it with a + or a −.
  3. Look at the overall picture without judgement. Where are there more "+"? Where are the "−"? Is any of it a surprise?
Example. "I wrote down ten people. It turned out three consistently give a '+', two consistently a '−', and the rest depend on the topic. And the surprise: the colleague I spend the most time with is almost always a '−'. Not because she's mean. Our conversations just come down to complaints."
Checkpoint: you have an honest picture of your circle, who fills you up and who drains you. Not so you can remove anyone, but so you can distribute your attention and time more consciously.
Exercise 2
Voices in your ears
mechanism: the media environment as part of your surroundings pain: "chronic anxiety, I can't stop"

Your surroundings aren't only living people. What you read, listen to and watch every day also shapes your field: quietly, unnoticed, but powerfully. Podcasts, social media, news, books, series: they're all voices constantly in your ears. And they're contagious too.

How to do it
  1. For one day, track what information you consume: which accounts, which topics, what tone. Don't evaluate, just note it.
  2. In the evening, ask: "what overall mood did today's information environment leave me with: anxiety, inspiration, emptiness, calm?"
  3. Identify one or two "voices" that regularly leave a negative residue, and one or two that inspire or calm you.
Example. "I follow twenty accounts. Most are anxious news or other people's successes that make me feel uneasy. Two inspire me: one author writes about science simply, another about craft. After them I want to do something. After the rest, I want to lie down."
Checkpoint: you know which media sources consistently take energy and which add it. And you have specific candidates for "less" and "more".
Exercise 3
The mirror of your environment
mechanism: identity through your surroundings · social mirroring pain: "I don't know who I am, I'm living someone else's life"

Your surroundings don't only affect your mood, they shape who you think you are. Different groups draw out different versions of you. This exercise helps you see which version of you your current field draws out, and whether that's the version you want to be.

How to do it
  1. Think of two or three different groups or environments in your life, current or past. Your work circle, friends, family, a sports team.
  2. For each, ask: "what kind of person do I become around them? Which of my qualities show up here, and which disappear?"
  3. Compare. Which version of you is closer to the one you want to be? In which environment does it appear?
Example. "With my colleagues at work I stay quiet, agree, never suggest anything. With my friends from university I laugh, overflow with ideas, stand my ground. The same person, two different fields, two different versions."
Checkpoint: you can name an environment where "your" version of you appears, and one where it gets lost. That's already knowledge you can do something with.
Level 2 · Core
Check the influence

Seeing the field isn't enough. You need to work out where your surroundings help you grow, and where they quietly keep you in place. And learn to protect what's yours.

Exercise 4
A field of growth or a field of stagnation
mechanism: assessing the environment as a factor in development pain: "I could do more, but I'm stuck in place"

Some environments expand you: in them you want to grow, try, take risks. Others narrow you: in them it's better to keep quiet and not stick your neck out. Everyone has both kinds, and that's normal. The question is which field takes up more of your time.

How to do it
  1. Assess your main work or social environment against three signs of growth: are new ideas encouraged here? Is a mistake a verdict or an experience? Is there someone nearby who is moving forward?
  2. Now the signs of stagnation: is "don't stick your neck out" the norm here? Is other people's success met with envy? Do conversations come down to complaints?
  3. Give an honest assessment: more growth or more stagnation? And what can you realistically do about it, even if you can't change the field completely?
Example. "A field of stagnation: at work people stay quiet, ideas aren't supported, complaining is the norm. But there are two signs of growth: one colleague I respect, and the chance to go to outside events. I'm starting with those, and at the same time looking for a different field."
Checkpoint: you've honestly assessed your main environment, and you know where it has even small points of growth. Even in a field of stagnation there almost always are some.
Exercise 5
The invisible rules of your circle
mechanism: awareness of absorbed social norms pain: "I'm living someone else's life, I always owe everyone"

Every circle has unspoken rules: what's "normal" here and what "isn't done". These rules are absorbed unconsciously and start to seem natural. But often behind them stands not your own value, but the norm of your surroundings.

How to do it
  1. Think about your main social circle. What things are "done" there? What things "aren't done"? Try to put the unspoken rules into words, the ones nobody ever says out loud.
  2. Now check each rule: "is this my value, or did I just take it from my environment?"
  3. Are there any rules among these that limit you, and that you'd like to let go of?
Example. "In our family you don't talk about money out loud. You don't boast about success. You don't ask for help, you have to cope on your own. I accepted this as the norm. And now I see that some of these rules get in my way, and they aren't mine."
Checkpoint: you've named at least one invisible rule of your circle and can say whether it's yours. A rule you're aware of no longer has the power of an invisible one.
Exercise 6
Comparison that inspires, and comparison that diminishes
mechanism: telling apart upward and downward social comparison pain: "I depend on other people's approval"

Comparing yourself with others is natural. But there are two different kinds: "I want the same, it shows what's possible" and "I'm nothing next to them". The first inspires, the second diminishes. The difference isn't in who you look at, but in what conclusion you draw about yourself.

How to do it
  1. Think of a person or an account that leaves you feeling worse. What conclusion about yourself do you draw after looking at them?
  2. Now think of a person who makes you want to act. What makes these two reactions different?
  3. Phrase your own rule: "when comparison inspires me, and when it diminishes me". This helps you choose more consciously whom to look at.
Example. "After one blogger I think 'I'll never be able to do that'. After another, 'oh, I could try that too'. The difference: the first shows the result without the path, the second shows the process, mistakes included. Now I know what to look for."
Checkpoint: in a specific moment you can tell whether this comparison inspires or diminishes you, and consciously choose what to do with it.
Exercise 7
A boundary as care
mechanism: practising boundaries without guilt · protecting your own field pain: "I say yes when I want to say no"

A boundary isn't a wall between you and another person. It's a line between what's yours and what isn't. It protects you both: you from exhaustion, the other person from a dishonest interaction. Saying "no" isn't cruelty. It's honesty.

How to do it
  1. Identify one situation where you regularly say "yes" when you want to say "no", and come out of it exhausted or annoyed with yourself.
  2. Phrase your boundary briefly and without apologising: "I can't", "that doesn't work for me", "not this time". No long explanations: explanations often sound like asking for permission.
  3. Try this phrase in the next similar situation. At least once. Even if it's awkward, do it and notice what happens.
Example. "A colleague keeps asking for last-minute help. I agree and stay until late at night. This time I said: 'I can't today, I've got plans.' No explanations. They found another way. Nothing fell apart."
Checkpoint: at least once you've said "no" without long explanations or apologies, and nothing terrible happened. The first boundary is the hardest. After that it gets easier.
Level 3 · Consolidation
Shape the field consciously

Don't wait for everything to change by itself. Make small conscious choices: add, reduce, create. Even where there isn't complete freedom.

Exercise 8
Add one voice
mechanism: widening your information and social field pain: "unfulfilled, stuck in place"

The smallest change to your field is adding one new voice that widens it. Not removing the old (that's harder), just letting something new in. One author, one podcast, one person. The field changes gradually, and often one new voice brings a whole new environment with it.

How to do it
  1. Work out what's missing in your current field: inspiration, calm, depth, movement, humour, wisdom.
  2. Find one specific source that gives it: an author, a podcast, a community, a person who is in your life but whom you rarely talk to.
  3. Add this voice to your regular environment, and keep it for at least a month before judging.
Example. "I was missing a sense of movement: everyone around me seemed to be standing still. I followed one author who documents their project from scratch, failures and steps included. After a month I noticed more 'I'll try it too'. One voice, and the field is a little different."
Checkpoint: the new voice has been in your field for a month, and you can name what specifically it gave you. Small, maybe, but noticeable.
Exercise 9
The minimum dose of contact
mechanism: adjusting the dose of contact without breaking the relationship pain: "no energy, chronic fatigue"

It isn't always possible, or necessary, to remove a person from your life. But you can reduce the dose of contact. Meeting less often, shorter conversations, less emotional involvement. That isn't indifference, it's regulating your own field without breaking the relationship.

How to do it
  1. Identify one person after whom you consistently feel drained, but whom you don't want to or can't "remove".
  2. Find where you can reduce the dose: meet less often, keep conversations shorter, don't reply straight away, don't share everything.
  3. Try the reduced dose for a month. Notice whether your state after these meetings has changed.
Example. "There's someone close to me who calls every day for an hour. I love them, but every day is too much. I started answering every other day and keeping it to 20 minutes. The relationship survived. I have noticeably more energy."
Checkpoint: you've found a way to reduce a draining contact, and the relationship didn't fall apart. Adjusting the dose isn't rejection. It's taking care of yourself within the relationship.
Exercise 10
Create your own field
mechanism: actively shaping your environment · social capital pain: "lonely among people, nobody understands"

The strongest move isn't looking for a ready-made field but starting to shape your own. Even a small community around something that matters to you, a couple of people heading in a similar direction, changes the field more than any outside conditions. You stop being a passive part of the field and become the one who sets it.

How to do it
  1. Work out what you'd like to have a community around: a topic, a value, a way of thinking, a kind of activity.
  2. Find or invite one or two people who share it. Not a "network", just specific people for specific conversations.
  3. Come up with the smallest format of regular contact: coffee once a month, a two-person chat, giving each other feedback on projects. Small but regular beats big and one-off.
Example. "I couldn't find people who think the way I do. I wrote to two acquaintances I find interesting to talk to and suggested meeting once a month. They agreed. Six months later, these two meetings had become the most nourishing part of my month."
Checkpoint: you have at least one regular point of contact with people around whom you feel you belong, and where the version of you that you want to be shows up.
How to tell the field is changing

Signs, not scores

The field changes gradually, and the changes aren't visible right away. Check in once a month.

You know who in your circle fills you up and who drains you, and you distribute your attention more consciously.
You notice the invisible rules of your environment and can tell which are yours and which are borrowed.
You can say "no" without long explanations, without damaging your relationships.
Your field has at least one voice that widens it, rather than just keeping the status quo.
There's at least one regular point of contact where you feel you belong.
Careful

What doesn't work with E

"Out with the toxic ones." Abruptly cutting people out of your life rarely brings relief and leaves guilt behind. What works more often is adjusting the dose and adding something new, not only removing the old.
Waiting for the perfect surroundings. They won't come. The field is always imperfect. The question is whether there's anything in it that widens you, and whether that "anything" can be increased.
Ignoring your media environment. Living people are only part of the field. What you consume every day shapes you just as much. Sometimes that's exactly the place to start.
To finish

Questions worth sitting with

1. Who do you become around the people who take up most of your time, and is that the version of you that you want to be?
2. Which invisible rules of your circle have you accepted without noticing, and would you sign up for them today?
3. Where in your life are there people or environments around whom "your" version of you appears?
4. What's easier for you: adding a new field or reducing an old one? Where will you start?
5. If you could consciously choose your surroundings for the next year, what would definitely be in them?
And finally

You're not a plant that can't choose its soil. You're a person who can consciously shape the field around you, even in small steps, even where there's little freedom.

One new voice. One boundary. One meeting with a person around whom you become yourself. The field changes gradually, but it does change.

What field are you starting to build today?

Svitlana Slipchenko, psychologist, author of Beyond the Horizon
