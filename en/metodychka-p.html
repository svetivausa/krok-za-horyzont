How to stop working against yourself

Ten exercises on the only component that can make your result negative

In the lecture on the Results Formula, B, Beliefs, was the trickiest letter. Because it's the only component that can turn the result not into zero but into a minus. Not "nothing worked out", but "I sabotage everything", without noticing how.

Imagine a filter on a camera lens. Everything you do, see and receive passes through it before it becomes a result. A clean filter, and the light comes through as it is. A cloudy one, and everything is in fog. And there's a filter that makes a negative: you try hard, and the opposite comes out.

B has two layers. Conscious beliefs are what you can put into words: "I won't manage", "I'm no good at public speaking". And identity sits deeper, at the level of "I am a person who…". It works automatically, you don't even hear this voice. But it's the one in charge.

A conscious belief can be challenged with facts. Identity changes only through new experience.

This workbook takes you through three levels: first seeing your filter, then testing it against reality, and finally, carefully, rewiring your identity. Not in one evening. But step by step.

Before you begin: the most important part
We are not "breaking" beliefs here, and we're not talking ourselves into "thinking positive". That doesn't work and only irritates. Many of your beliefs once protected you, and they deserve respect, not war. We're simply learning to see them and to test them honestly. If an exercise brings up strong pain, that's not a command to "push through". It's a signal to slow down, breathe out, and if needed reach out for support to people close to you or to a professional. Take care of yourself. There's no rush here.
How it works

How to use this workbook

1
This is work, not reading. Beliefs don't change from reading about them. Only from an honest look at yourself and small actions.
2
In order, without rushing. First learn to see the filter, only then test and change it. There's no point skipping ahead.
3
With curiosity, not judgement. The main tool here isn't willpower but curiosity. "Oh, interesting, where does this come from in me?" works better than "how awful of me".
4
Tick an exercise when it has become yours. Not when you've read it, but when you've actually done it. The progress bar at the top shows the path you've walked.
Level 1 · Foundation
See the filter

The hardest thing about beliefs is that we don't experience them as beliefs. They just seem like "the truth about me". The first step is learning to notice them.

Exercise 1
Catch the "I" thought
mechanism: awareness of automatic thoughts pain: "my self-esteem depends on others"

Throughout the day your brain keeps commenting on you: quickly, quietly, almost unnoticed. "Late again." "Who am I to try." "Good thing nobody saw." These thoughts fly by in a fraction of a second, but they are what shapes the filter. First you simply need to hear them.

How to do it
  1. Set 3–4 reminders on your phone during the day, at random moments.
  2. When one goes off, stop and ask: "what thought about myself just flashed by?" Write it down word for word, even if it's tiny.
  3. Don't judge or correct. Just catch and write. Like an entomologist building a collection, without judgement.
Example: "11:00: 'I always overcomplicate things.' 15:30: 'nobody takes me seriously.' 19:00: 'I'm a bad mother because I'm tired.'"
Checkpoint: by the end of the week you have a list of 10–15 "I" thoughts. You notice with surprise that some repeat almost word for word. That's your filter, visible for the first time.
Exercise 2
Marker words
mechanism: linguistic signals of rigid beliefs pain: "whatever I do, it never feels like enough"

Rigid beliefs have recognisable flag words. "Always", "never", "everyone", "no one", "I'm that kind of person", "again", "as usual". When you use them about yourself, it's almost certainly not a fact but a belief pretending to be one.

How to do it
  1. Take your list of "I" thoughts from exercise 1. Underline the marker words: always, never, everyone, no one, as usual.
  2. Rewrite each underlined phrase more honestly, with specifics. "I always fail at everything" → "this time this particular thing didn't work out".
  3. Feel the difference in your body. The first phrase presses down, the second leaves room to breathe.
Example: "Nobody listens to me" → "at today's meeting I was interrupted twice". The first is a life sentence. The second is a specific event you can do something about.
Checkpoint: you start catching "always/never" right in speech, yours and other people's, and something inside goes "stop, that's a belief, not a fact".
Exercise 3
A map of the filter
mechanism: locating the areas of distortion pain: "I could do more, but I'm stuck in place"

The filter is rarely equally cloudy everywhere. At work you may feel confident, and in relationships not. Or the other way round. This exercise shows where exactly your filter distorts the most, so you know where to look.

How to do it
  1. Write down 5–6 areas of life: work, money, body, relationships, parenting, friendship, creativity.
  2. Next to each, give an honest score: how strongly "I can't do it / I'm not that kind of person / I'm not allowed" kicks in here, from 0 to 10.
  3. Circle the two areas with the highest scores. Those are your cloudiest zones. That's what we'll work with next.
Example: "Work: 3 (I'm fine here). Money: 8 ('I'm just not made for earning'). Body: 7. Relationships: 4." It's clear at once: the work is with money and body, not with "my whole life".
Checkpoint: you have two specific target areas instead of a vague feeling that "something is wrong with me". Specifics are already half the relief.
Level 2 · Core
Test it and loosen it

Seeing a belief isn't enough. Now the main thing: check it against reality. Because most limiting beliefs don't survive an honest look at the facts.

Exercise 4
Fact or interpretation
mechanism: separating an event from its evaluation pain: "whatever I do, it never feels like enough"

Between what happened and what you decided about yourself because of it lies a gulf. "They didn't call me back" is a fact. "Because I'm boring" is already an interpretation the filter painted in. This exercise teaches you to cut cleanly between the two.

How to do it
  1. Take a situation that stung you this week. Describe it in two columns.
  2. Left column: facts only. What literally happened, what a camera would record. No "because" and no "which means".
  3. Right column: interpretations. What you decided about yourself. Now honestly: is that the only possible explanation? Or did the filter pick the worst one?
Example. Fact: "I texted a friend, they didn't reply for two days." Interpretation: "they're not interested in me, I'm just clingy." And reality? They could have been busy, ill, or simply forgot. The evidence for "I'm clingy" is zero.
Checkpoint: you start asking automatically "is this a fact, or did I add it?", and you often catch that the pain comes from the interpretation, not from the event itself.
Exercise 5
Calibrating with facts
mechanism: looking for counter-evidence · testing a hypothesis pain: "my mood depends on how others rate me"

A belief behaves like a biased judge: it notices only the evidence "for" and doesn't see the evidence "against". This exercise restores the balance: you deliberately look for facts that contradict the belief. And they almost always turn up.

How to do it
  1. Take one limiting belief from your filter map (exercise 3). Write it at the top of a page.
  2. Now be the defence lawyer, not the prosecutor: write down every fact from your life that contradicts this belief. Even small ones. Even old ones.
  3. Look at the list. Does the belief still sound like "the plain truth", or more like an exaggeration?
Example. Belief: "I can't finish things." Counter-evidence: finished my studies, carried a pregnancy to term, saw a renovation through, finished a thick book last year. It turns out you do finish things, more than once. The truth is closer to: "I find it hard with things I don't really want to do."
Checkpoint: the old belief no longer sounds absolute. "Sometimes yes, sometimes no" appears, and that's freedom instead of a verdict.
Exercise 6
Whose voice is this
mechanism: attributing the source of a belief pain: "I live on autopilot, I don't know what I want"

Most deep beliefs about yourself aren't yours. Someone said them once, and you took them as truth because you were small and couldn't argue. When you listen to the voice saying it, it almost never sounds like your own.

How to do it
  1. Take the belief you're working with. Close your eyes and say it quietly out loud a few times.
  2. Listen: whose voice is this? The intonation, the pace, the words: who does it sound like? Mum, dad, a teacher, someone from childhood?
  3. When you recognise it, say in your mind: "this is your thought, not mine. Thank you, I'll take it from here." No war. You're simply handing back what belongs to someone else.
Example. "Don't stick your neck out, they'll laugh at you", and suddenly you hear your grandmother's intonation. It was her survival strategy in her time. It protected her. But it's her fear, not yours, and not necessarily your truth today.
Checkpoint: the belief stops feeling like "the truth about me" and starts feeling like "someone else's voice I've been carrying". And someone else's voice can be turned down.
Exercise 7
What this belief once protected
mechanism: acknowledging the protective function · self-compassion pain: "guilt, no inner support"

This is the deepest exercise of the level. No belief appeared "just like that". Once it protected you: from pain, from shame, from danger. If you simply declare it "the enemy" and try to rip it out by the roots, your psyche will resist. So first comes gratitude, and only then letting go.

How to do it
  1. Take the belief that gets in your way the most. Ask it gently: "what did you once protect me from?"
  2. Answer honestly. Perhaps "don't stick your neck out" protected you from humiliation. "Don't trust anyone" from betrayal. "Be easy to deal with" from a conflict that was dangerous.
  3. Thank this belief for its work. And then quietly say: "thank you, but now I'm an adult and I'll manage differently." It's easier to let go of something you first understood rather than hated.
Example. "I have to be easy for everyone" once protected a child from an adult's anger. Back then it was wise. Today it takes away life. You can thank that strategy and choose a new, adult one.
Checkpoint: towards the old belief you feel not anger but something like gratitude and calm. That's a sign it no longer runs you from the shadows.
Level 3 · Consolidation
Rewire your identity

The deepest level. A conscious belief can be tested with facts. But identity, who you consider yourself to be at the level of "I am", changes only through new lived experience.

Exercise 8
An audit of "I am a person who…"
mechanism: uncovering the identity layer pain: "I don't know who I really am"

Here we reach the deepest layer, the one that runs in the background and steers without your consent. It hides in the phrase "I am a person who…". What you write after the tenth repetition will tell you more about yourself than hours of thinking.

How to do it
  1. Take a sheet of paper. Write "I am a person who…" and finish the sentence 20 times in a row. Fast, no editing, no censoring.
  2. The first 5–7 will be predictable ("who loves coffee"). Don't stop. The interesting part starts after the tenth.
  3. Underline the ones that make something tighten inside. That's your identity, on paper for the first time.
Example. After the tenth line, out comes: "I am a person nobody listens to", "who always has to carry everything", "who has no right to be tired". That's where the filter lives at its deepest level.
Checkpoint: you can name 2–3 identity statements that were working in you unnoticed. Once named out loud, they already lose some of their power.
Exercise 9
Action ahead of belief
mechanism: changing identity through behaviour · self-perception pain: "I say yes when I want to say no"

The main trap: we wait until we believe in ourselves before we act. But it works the other way round. The brain draws its conclusion about "who I am" from what you do. So identity isn't changed by affirmations, but by a small action taken before the belief arrives.

How to do it
  1. Take the identity you want to have instead of the old one. Not "confident" in general, but specifically: "a person who speaks their mind".
  2. Come up with the smallest possible action on behalf of this new identity. So small you'll definitely do it. Say one opinion at one meeting.
  3. Do it, even though you don't yet "feel like that kind of person". Action first, belief will catch up.
Example. Old identity: "I'm easy-going, I stay quiet." New one: "I'm a person with a voice." Smallest action: at a family dinner, say which film you want to watch. A small thing. But the brain records: "oh, it turns out I'm someone who speaks up."
Checkpoint: after a few such micro-actions you catch a moment when you acted the new way without an inner fight. That's your new identity taking its first independent step.
Exercise 10
A new statement about yourself
mechanism: consolidating the new identity on facts pain: "I need others to recognise me"

The final exercise. Here you gather everything you've done into one new statement about yourself. Not an empty affirmation like "I'm the best", but an honest statement backed by real facts and actions from the previous exercises. One you can believe, because it's true.

How to do it
  1. Take the old identity (exercise 8) and the new one you're growing (exercise 9).
  2. Phrase the new statement and add evidence to it. Not "I'm a confident person", but "I'm a person who's learning to speak up, and I've already said what I think twice this week".
  3. Write it where you'll see it every day. Reread it not as a spell, but as a statement of a fact that keeps accumulating.
Example. Instead of "I'm worthless" (the old one) and instead of "I'm a genius" (an empty affirmation), something honest and backed by facts: "I'm a person who has finished many things and is learning to finish even the ones that scare me." It's believable, because the evidence from exercise 5 stands behind it.
Checkpoint: you say your new statement about yourself, and the familiar "oh come on, who am I kidding" no longer flares up inside. There's nothing to argue with, because the statement rests on real facts, not on fiction. That's your new, honest foundation.
How to tell the filter is clearing

Signs, not scores

B can't be measured in numbers. But there are signs that the filter is becoming clearer. Check in with them once a month.

You catch the words "always / never / I'm that kind of person" right in your speech, and something inside goes "stop, that's a belief".
A pause appears between an event and your conclusion about yourself: "is this a fact, or did I add it?"
Old beliefs no longer sound like the truth, but like someone else's voice that can be turned down.
Sometimes you act the new way without an inner fight: the new identity takes steps by itself.
Self-compassion appears instead of self-criticism. This may be the main sign.
Careful

What doesn't work with B

Three common traps that only strengthen the old filter.

Positive thinking as self-deception. Repeating "I'm rich" when inside you don't believe it: the brain hears a lie and resists even harder. Statements need to rest on facts.
Pushing through pain. If an exercise brings up something sharp, that's no reason to "grit your teeth and force it". Slow down, and if needed, reach out for support.
Fighting yourself. "How awful of me to think like this" is the same filter, just from the other side. Curiosity works, not self-hatred.
To finish

Questions worth sitting with

1. Which belief about myself have I considered "just the truth" for the longest, when it turned out to be someone else's voice?
2. What did my limitations once protect me from, and do I need that protection today?
3. Who would I be if I didn't believe the worst things I think about myself?
4. What small action from my new identity can I take today?
5. What would change in my result by the formula if B turned from a minus into a plus?
And finally

Beliefs are the quietest and most powerful component of the formula. You can't see them, but they decide whether all your work gets multiplied by a plus or by a minus.

You don't need to remake yourself in one evening. It's enough to start noticing your filter, and to treat it with curiosity rather than war. The rest will come, step by step.

Whose voice will you finally turn down?

Svitlana Slipchenko, psychologist, author of Beyond the Horizon
