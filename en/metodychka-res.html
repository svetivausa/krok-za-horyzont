How to fill yourself up, not just spend

Ten exercises on the component that powers the whole Results Formula

In the Results Formula, Res (resources) is the fuel. And if it's at zero, the rest of the formula doesn't matter: even the right direction, even a living desire, even the strongest identity won't move without fuel.

Resources come in two kinds. Internal: energy, attention, time, mental resilience, the ability to take a hit and come back. External: money, connections, tools, access to the information you need. Both matter. And neither is fixed.

Here's the most important thing to understand about resources: they aren't given by default. You build them yourself. Every action comes back to you as new fuel: a new skill, a new contact, a little more resilience. And tomorrow you start from a bit more. It's a loop. But for it to start, you need at least some minimum for the first step.

Not "where do I get the resources to start". But "what do I start so the resources begin to build up".
Before you begin
There are situations where the resource is truly drained to zero: illness, grief, war nearby, years of chronic exhaustion. In those situations no "technique" can replace live support: a person beside you, a professional, time. If you feel empty to the very bottom and can't move, that's not weakness and not failure. It's a signal to reach out for help, not to do one more exercise.

Level 1 · Foundation
See the fuel

Before filling up, take an honest look at how much fuel is left and where it's leaking. Without this, any "recovery" is patching holes at random.

Exercise 1
Resource thermometer
mechanism: awareness of your current resource level pain: "worn out by all the 'shoulds', no strength left for what matters"

We rarely stop to simply and honestly ask: how much fuel do I have? It's habitual to run on fumes without noticing the tank is almost empty. This snapshot is your starting point.

How to do it
  1. Rate each kind of resource from 0 to 10: physical energy, emotional resource, attention (the ability to focus), sense of time (that you have it and are in charge of it), mental resilience.
  2. Write the numbers down honestly: not "how I'd like it to be", but how it is right now.
  3. Look at the whole picture. Where's the lowest number? What does it say about where your system is most drained?
Example. "Physical: 6, emotional: 3, attention: 4, time: 2, resilience: 5. Lowest are time and emotions. No wonder it's hard to get moving: I'm constantly switching and have nowhere to recover emotionally."
Checkpoint: you have an honest snapshot of your resources, and you can see what's most drained. It's not a verdict. It's a map you can work with.
Exercise 2
Where it leaks
mechanism: identifying chronic sources of exhaustion pain: "tired all the time, nothing left over"

A resource rarely leaks through one big hole. More often it's a dozen small ones, each of them "nothing special". But together they keep the tank half empty all the time. This exercise helps you find those "holes": the chronic, regular drains.

How to do it
  1. Think about last week. What regularly took more than it gave? Not disasters, everyday things: unfinished tasks, tense relationships, anxious thoughts in the background, information noise.
  2. Write down 5–7 such "leaks". No judgement, just note them.
  3. Mark which of them you can stop or reduce. And which you can't yet, but it matters to at least see them.
Example. "An unfinished conversation with my partner hums in the background all day. News every hour, and the anxiety builds up. Email with no limits, and my attention is scattered. Three things, and the tank is half empty before lunch."
Checkpoint: you know your main "holes", and you can shrink at least one of them this week. Even a small reduction in a leak is an increase in your resource.
Exercise 3
Signals of depletion
mechanism: interoception · recognising early signs of exhaustion pain: "chronic anxiety, I can't stop"

A resource rarely runs out suddenly. The body and your behaviour warn you long before the critical point, but we've learned to ignore them. This exercise teaches you to recognise your personal early signals, so you can respond while there's still something to restore.

How to do it
  1. Recall a moment when you were truly exhausted. What signs appeared before things got really bad?
  2. Write down your personal "early signals": physical (tension, insomnia, headaches), behavioural (irritability, procrastination, eating without hunger), emotional (helplessness, cynicism, detachment).
  3. Define your "yellow level": two or three signals after which you need to stop and recover, without waiting for red.
Example. "My early signals: I start snapping at small things, stop answering messages, wake up at four and think about work. Yellow level is when two of the three are there. I used to wait for all three. Now I react to two."
Checkpoint: you know your "yellow level", and next time it goes off, you have a plan: stop and recover, rather than push on to red.
Level 2 · Core
Stop the leak

Before filling up, stop the drain. There's no point pouring water into a leaky bucket. Here we remove what constantly drains you, and learn to truly recover.

Exercise 4
Plug one "hole"
mechanism: reducing chronic exhaustion · freeing up resource pain: "no strength for what matters"

From your list of "leaks" in exercise 2, pick one hole and plug it. Not all of them at once. One. Sometimes the simplest change gives a disproportionately big boost, because it stops a chronic background drain.

How to do it
  1. From exercise 2, pick one leak you can realistically reduce: not eliminate perfectly, just make smaller.
  2. Phrase a specific change: not "less news", but "I check the news only twice a day, morning and evening".
  3. Keep this change for a week. Note whether you have more resource, even a little.
Example. "The hole: email with no limits, I check it constantly, my attention is scattered. The change: email three times a day, at 9:00, 13:00 and 17:00. The rest of the time it's closed. Within a week I felt it: my thoughts got more focused, and I got more done in the same time."
Checkpoint: you plugged one chronic hole for a week, and felt the difference. Even a small one is a sign that the loop has started to close.
Exercise 5
Guarding your attention
mechanism: managing attention as a resource · cognitive load pain: "living on autopilot, my head never rests"

Attention is the scarcest resource. Money can be earned, sleep can be caught up on. But attention scattered across a hundred stimuli is much harder to restore. And the modern world is designed to take it from you constantly. Guarding your attention isn't a luxury, it's a necessity.

How to do it
  1. Find the main "attention eaters" in your day: notifications, switching between tasks, background conversations, unfinished things left open.
  2. Pick one protective measure: turn off notifications for a block of work, close unnecessary tabs, put on headphones, set a specific time for "switching".
  3. Try that one measure for a week. Notice how the quality of your attention changes.
Example. "I turned off all phone notifications except calls. Day one felt awkward, my hand kept reaching for the phone. By day three I noticed my thoughts had become longer and I was working more deeply. Attention is a muscle, and it started to rest."
Checkpoint: you have at least one daily block where your attention is protected from interruptions. Even 30 minutes a day is a different quality of thinking.
Exercise 6
Rest that actually restores
mechanism: telling real rest apart from mere "switching" pain: "I rest, and I'm still tired"

Some things feel like rest but only switch you over, they don't restore you. Scrolling social media, a series on in the background, a conversation about nothing: your attention is occupied, but the resource doesn't grow. Real rest is different for everyone. This exercise helps you find yours.

How to do it
  1. Recall 3–4 things you do "to rest". After each, ask: "Do I have more strength after this, or about the same?"
  2. Separate "switching" (attention is occupied but not recharging) from "restoring" (afterwards you really do have more resource).
  3. Name 1–2 things that truly restore you specifically. And schedule them like an important meeting, not "if there's time left".
Example. "A series is switching, I feel about the same afterwards. A walk without my phone is restoring, my head is clearer afterwards. Cooking in silence is restoring: my hands are busy, my head rests. Now I schedule the walk like a work meeting, in my calendar."

Exercise 7
A minimum protective ritual
mechanism: micro-routines of recovery · regular refuelling pain: "no time for myself"

One big recovery a year (a holiday, a retreat, "I'll drop everything") gives less than a small protective ritual every day. The aim of this exercise is to find the smallest regular action for yourself that keeps your resource from total depletion.

How to do it
  1. Come up with one ritual that takes 5–15 minutes and genuinely refuels you. Not "the perfect hour-long morning", but the minimum protection that's enough.
  2. Tie it to an anchor (as in Workbook A): "after my morning coffee", "before bed", "after work".
  3. Treat this ritual as non-negotiable, even on the busiest days. Those are exactly the days it matters most.
Example. "My minimum ritual: 10 minutes in the morning without my phone, just coffee and quiet. Nothing more. When it's there, the day feels different. When I skip it, I notice. It's the least I can do for myself every day."
Checkpoint: you have one minimum ritual that holds even on hard days, and you feel the difference between the days when it's there and when it isn't.
Level 3 · Consolidation
Build up and multiply

A resource doesn't just recover, it grows. Every action comes back as new fuel. This level is about starting the self-renewing loop.

Exercise 8
An action that comes back as resource
mechanism: the β + Res loop · building up through action pain: "I'm standing still, I don't see results"

The most interesting thing about resources is that they grow from action. You had a hard conversation, and you gained resilience. You did something new, and you gained a skill. You got through a failure and took a lesson from it, and you gained understanding. That's the loop: a result comes back as resource. This exercise helps you see it.

How to do it
  1. Recall three things you've done in recent months, even small ones, even imperfectly.
  2. For each, ask: "What did this action give back to me? What skill, resilience, contact, understanding?"
  3. Write down these "returns", and notice: your resource has already grown. You just didn't always see it.
Example. "A year ago I took on my first paid project, and I was terrified. What came back? The confidence that I can do it, real negotiating experience, and one client who recommended me further. Three resources from one action, and I never counted them."
Checkpoint: you can see that your actions have already come back as resource, and you're starting to track these "returns" consciously. That's the β + Res loop in action.
Exercise 9
An audit of external resources
mechanism: taking stock of the social and material capital you already have pain: "no one to do it with, nothing to do it with, nowhere to get it"

External resources (money, connections, tools, information) are often there but go unnoticed or unused. People you could call but don't. Tools lying idle. Knowledge you have but don't apply. This exercise is an inventory of what you already have.

How to do it
  1. Go through four kinds of external resources: people (who could help if you asked), tools (what you already have but don't use), knowledge (what information you have access to but ignore), finances (is there anything you could redistribute).
  2. For each kind, write down 1–2 specific unused resources.
  3. Pick one, and activate it this week. Not "I'll ask someday", but specifically and now.
Example. "People: I know someone in the field I want to move into, and I've never once written to them. Tools: a subscription I don't use. Knowledge: a course I bought a year ago and never opened. I'm starting with the person, writing to them this week."
Checkpoint: you've activated at least one external resource that used to lie idle. The inventory showed you have more resources than it seemed.
Exercise 10
The flywheel
mechanism: starting the self-renewing loop · building up through small steps pain: "unfulfilled, waiting for better conditions"

A flywheel is a heavy mechanism. The first turns take effort, but with every turn it speeds up on its own. It's the same with resources: the first step takes more than it gives. But if you don't stop, the loop picks up speed, and the resource starts to grow by itself. The final exercise is to start your flywheel.

How to do it
  1. Choose one area where you want to start a loop of growing resource. It could be physical energy, resilience, social capital, a financial cushion.
  2. Define the smallest first turn of the flywheel: one small regular action that will come back as resource over time. Not "a radical change", a first step.
  3. Take it, and make a deal with yourself: three months without judging the result. A flywheel picks up speed slowly. Stopping after a week because "nothing has changed" means never really starting it.
Example. "I want more physical energy. The flywheel: 20 minutes of movement every day, any kind. The first weeks, I forced myself. After a month, I notice I sleep better. After three, I have more energy in the evening. The flywheel is spinning."
Checkpoint: you've kept up the first turn of the flywheel for three months, and you notice it's starting to spin more easily. Resources grow not from inspiration, but from regularity.
How to tell your resource is growing

Signs, not numbers

You can't measure resource in points. But there are signs that the loop has started. Check in once a month.

You know your "yellow level" of exhaustion and respond before things get critical.
You have a minimum protective ritual that holds even on hard days.
You notice how actions come back as resource: as a skill, resilience, a connection.
Your week has real rest in it: not switching, but something that truly restores you.
The flywheel is spinning: there's one area where your resource grows steadily thanks to a small regular action.
Careful

What doesn't work with Res

Waiting for "the big recovery". A holiday once a year, "once I've rested, I'll start": it doesn't work. A resource is sustained by small regular actions, not rare big ones.
Filling up without stopping the leak. If the chronic "holes" aren't plugged, no recovery has a lasting effect. First, stop the drain.
Ignoring your body's signals. "I'll push through" is the most expensive strategy. Reacting early at the "yellow level" costs far less than recovering after "red".
To finish

Questions worth sitting with

1. Where is the biggest leak of resource in my life right now, and can I reduce it?
2. What truly restores me specifically, and when did I last do it?
3. Which of my actions have already come back as resource, without me noticing?
4. Which external resources do I have that are lying idle?
5. Which flywheel do I want to start, and what's the smallest first turn for it?
And finally

A resource isn't something you either have or don't from birth. It's something you build every day: with small actions, protective rituals, conscious choices. And it comes back to you bigger each time.

Not "where do I find the strength". But "what do I start so that it begins to build up". The first turn of the flywheel is the hardest. But it's also the most important.
