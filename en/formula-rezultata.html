The Results Formula

Why it works out for some and not for others. Seven letters, one loop and one truth.

At school, everyone thought I was unlucky.

If there were twenty exam tickets, I'd somehow manage to draw the one I hadn't had time to review. If a contest had one bad option, it went to me. My friends would laugh: “Only you could pull that off.” And honestly, I believed it too.

Because the bad luck wasn't only in small things. My father drank. There were often fights at home. Money barely covered the basics. And our neighborhood had such a reputation that even grown men avoided going there after dark. It seemed that in the lottery of life, I'd drawn far from the best ticket.

And you know what's interesting? Back then I looked at other people and thought luck decided everything. Some got good parents. Some got money. Some got connections. Some got self-confidence from childhood. Then the years went by, and I started seeing strange things.

People who seemed luckier than me didn't always get far in life. Some just kept waiting for the next bit of luck. The next opportunity. The next happy accident. And those who weren't especially lucky learned not to rely on luck. They learned to look for a way out. They learned to get back up. They learned to take a step when there were no guarantees.

And that's when I understood something. Maybe it was never about luck. Luck can give you a good start, but it can't walk the road for you. From then on, I stopped waiting for a lucky ticket and started looking for something else: what really separates those who get there from those who stay in place. I searched for years, in books, in people, in my own falls and comebacks. And in the end, what I was looking for came together into a formula.

There is a formula. Not a “formula for success” from a glossy cover. A real one: an equation that shows what your result is actually made of.

It has seven components, one loop and one background. If even one component becomes zero, the whole result is zero. If one variable goes negative, the formula works against you. And if everything is positive, then over time… this is where it gets interesting. Over time, the exponential kicks in.

Today I'll lay it out in full. I'll decode every letter. You'll sit down and see why you got stuck exactly where you got stuck, and where the point is that you need to press.

Let's sit down. Let's figure it out.

Here it is

What the formula looks like

Don't be scared of the letters. In a moment I'll translate each one into human language, with a kitchen, a car, a camera and a key in the ignition. For now, just look.

Π · Rules of the game
laws of nature · social norms · the rules of your field · Greek capital “pi”
The engine · the small result r you produce at every moment
r(t) = ( D × B(i·c) ) · A(q·d·f) × E(c·ch) × O(n·u) × Res(t) ± ε(t)
The loop · how the result comes back into you
Res(t+1) = Res(t) + β · r(t)
The total · the big Result over the whole time T
R(T) = Σ r(t)

D is Desire, B is Beliefs, A is Action, E is Environment, O is Opportunities, Res is Resources. The small letters in brackets are the parts each component is made of; we'll get to them.

Let me explain one detail right away, because it matters. There are two “r”s in the formula. The small r is your result for one step: what you do today, this week, in this particular thing. The big R is the big Result, your goal: what you're doing it all for.

And here's the main thing: the big R is made of small r's. A big result doesn't fall from the sky ready-made. It's assembled step by step from small daily results. The “Σ” sign (Greek for “sum”) means exactly that: “add up all your small steps over all the time, and you get the big goal.” No dream exists separately from what you do today.

Seems complicated? That's normal. Now we'll take each letter in turn and look at what it means, where it hides in your everyday life, and what to do with it.

Before we dive in, one rule you need to understand first, because without it nothing else adds up.

The first law, which explains everything

Everything multiplies. Nothing adds up.

Look at the formula again. There isn't a single plus between the components. Only multiplication. That's no accident.

Most models of success you see are additive: “a little motivation + a little discipline + a little luck = results.” Sounds reassuring. And it's not true.

Imagine you're making your favorite soup. Seven ingredients. If you forget the salt, how much worse is the soup? By 1/7? No. It'll be tasteless. Completely. And if you forget the vegetables, there's no soup at all. Cooking doesn't add ingredients. It multiplies them.

Any component = 0 → the whole result = 0.

That sounds harsh. And it's exactly why some people with every outward sign of success, wealth, degrees, family, have a pit inside. One of their seven is at zero. Most often the zero hides in one of three places. Either the desire isn't really theirs but imposed. Or a belief is working against them. Or their inner resources are exhausted, and the engine simply has nothing to run on.

The good news: this same property works the other way too. You don't need to bring all seven components to perfection. You need none of them to be zero. Then everything multiplies and produces a result.

Check right now: think of an area where you're stuck. Not “where something's a bit off”, but where you're truly spinning your wheels even though you seem to be doing everything. Figuratively speaking, I'd bet exactly one component has hit zero there. Just one. Try to find it.
D
Desire
ignition coefficient · [0 → 1]

Without this letter, nothing starts. Like a car without a key in the ignition. You can have the best engine, a full tank, open roads, and the car just sits there.

At first it seems your Desire is fine. Who doesn't want things? One woman wants breakfast, another wants her own business, a third wants her husband to finally understand what she wants from him. A whole fridge full of “I want”s. So why isn't the result coming?

Because not every “wanting” is actually Desire.

In the formula, D is a coefficient from zero to one. Zero means you don't really want it (even if you tell yourself you do). One is when your body moves in that direction on its own. You don't bargain with yourself every morning, you just get up and do it. Not “I should go to the gym”, but your sneakers are already on.

There's a discovery in neuroscience that once stunned me. In the 90s, Kent Berridge showed that “wanting” and “liking” are two different signals in the brain. Wanting runs on dopamine. Liking runs on a different set of chemicals and brain areas. And they can work separately. That's why a person addicted to cigarettes wants to smoke even when the taste stopped being pleasant long ago: the “wanting” signal stayed, the “liking” faded.

True Desire in the formula is when both signals match. Wanting and liking. When they diverge, you either chase something that brings you no joy (and when you get it, you feel empty), or you don't move at all, because you don't really want it.

In the same way, a person can scroll their phone feed for months, wanting it every time even though the pleasure is long gone. “Wanting” pulls the hand to the screen, while “liking” has fallen silent. The two signals drifted apart, and the habit stayed.

You can spend your whole life achieving things you never truly wanted. Just because someone once said you “should”.

Think of something very familiar. Someone keeps saying for years: “I want to finally find time for myself.” They mean it. But as soon as a free hour appears, an urgent task turns up, someone's request, something “more important”. It's not laziness or hypocrisy. It's that real Desire here is weaker than the habit of being needed by everyone except yourself. In words, D is close to one; in reality, it's somewhere near zero.

Sometimes we simply confuse our desire with someone else's. You say, “I want to learn English”, but what you really want is not to fall behind colleagues who travel to conferences abroad. As soon as that pressure disappears, you change jobs, there are different people around, and the “desire” to learn the language disappears too. So it was never about the language. It was about not feeling left behind. These are different things, and it's important not to mix them up.

The authenticity test
Imagine the goal has already been reached, and no one will ever know about it. Not your mom, not social media, not your old classmate. Do you still want it? If yes, your D is real. If it's “then what's the point?”, it isn't yours. And there's no need to torture yourself.
D in short: Desire isn't “I should”. It's when your body moves that way on its own. If it doesn't, the question isn't “how do I force myself” but “is this really mine”.
B
Beliefs
a filter · two layers: i (identity) · c (conscious beliefs) · [−1 → +1]

This is the trickiest letter in the formula. And the only one that can make your result negative. Not “nothing will work out”, but “it'll work out backwards, into the minus”. You'll literally be working against yourself, and not notice.

B is a filter. Like on a camera. All the information about the world, all your actions, all your resources pass through it before they become a result. A clean filter (B close to +1), and light passes through as it is, the picture is clear. A murky one (B close to 0), and everything looks foggy. A black one (B = −1), and the camera makes a negative: everything you do comes back to you distorted.

B has two layers. I mark them “i” for identity and “c” for conscious beliefs. They're not the same thing, and that matters.

c · your conscious beliefs

These are the things you can put into words. “I believe you can make money doing what you love.” “I think I don't stand a chance at a senior position because I'm from a small town.” This layer is easier to notice and change. You read a book, see someone else's example, and the belief shifts.

i · your identity

This is the deeper layer. Not “I think that…”, but “I am a person who…”. “I'm a person who always stays in the shadows.” “I'm a person who isn't trusted with important things.” “I'm a person who's at peace with everyone, but has nothing of their own.” It works automatically. You don't notice how you walk into your 39th job interview without confidence: your body knows the script, your bones remember. It's not a “thought”. It is you (or so you believe).

Conscious beliefs can be rewritten. Identity has to be lived anew.

And this is why B can go negative. If your identity says “I'm a person things never go well for”, and you suddenly start doing everything right and good things start coming, your brain perceives it as a threat. It defends your picture of the world. And it arranges sabotage. You're late for an important meeting (even though you're never late). You “accidentally” offend the person who could have opened a door. You get sick the day before. It's not “karma”, it's your identity protecting itself.

Neuroscience describes this through systems that work in the brain without your consent. The anterior cingulate cortex keeps checking: “does what's happening now match who I believe I am?” If not, the amygdala sounds the alarm, and you unconsciously do something to get back to the familiar. Even if the familiar is pain.

The identity test
Take a sheet of paper. Write “I am a person who…” and finish the sentence 20 times. Quickly, without editing. The first 5–7 will be predictable (“I am a person who loves reading”). It gets interesting after the tenth. That's where what runs in the background comes out: “I am a person no one listens to”, “I am a person who always has to carry everything”. These are the phrases that show you what you really believe about yourself: your deep B.
B in short: If you do everything right and the result still crawls into the minus, the question isn't “what else should I do”. It's “what do I really believe about myself”. This can be healed. Not in one evening. But it can be healed.
A
Action
q (quality) × d (direction) × f (frequency)

This letter is the biggest trick in the whole formula. Because it's also the only one that's fully in your hands right now. Not “once I sort out my psychological issues”. Not “once I have the energy”. Now.

Why a trick? Because it seems that Action just means “doing a lot”. In fact, A has three parameters, and if even one is zero, actions don't produce results. Like the whole formula, A is multiplication inside too.

q · quality

Not “doing a lot” but “doing it well”. A hundred emails written carelessly will give you less than ten written with heart. One hour-long workout where you're fully in it replaces three where you lift at half strength. This isn't about perfectionism. It's about the minimum threshold below which an action stops working.

d · direction

Where exactly you're aiming. You can be the best shooter in the world, but if it's the wrong target, it's all for nothing. I know a person who spent ten years flawlessly building a career in a field that was burning them out. The actions were perfect. The direction wasn't. In a case like that, efficiency works against you: you just get faster to where you don't want to be.

f · frequency

Regularity. One brilliant workout a month doesn't get you in shape. Five average ones do. What works here isn't “heroic effort” but “often enough not to drop out”. Both the brain and the body learn through repetition over time, not through the intensity of a single day.

Now picture a car. Quality is whether it runs at all or is falling apart. Direction is where you turn the wheel. Frequency is how often you start the engine. You can have a great car with perfect steering and leave it in the garage all year. You can drive out every day, but toward a place where there's nothing. You can drive in the right direction in an old wreck that's about to fall apart. In each of these cases there seems to be plenty of action, but you rarely arrive.

Action is the only variable you can change in the next five minutes. Not “starting Monday”. Now.

One more thing. Most people get stuck on A not because they're lazy. But because they wait for motivation or inspiration to start. In neuroscience this is sometimes called a “sequence error”: we think motivation → action. But more often it's action → motivation. You do the first 10 minutes, and the “desire to keep going” shows up. You wait for inspiration, and it never comes.

An audit of your A
Take one week. Write down everything you do during working hours. Now make three columns: quality (how much I like it), direction (does it bring me closer to what I really want), frequency (do I do it systematically). Whatever gets a plus in all three columns is your real A. The rest is an illusion of activity.
A in short: The question isn't “how do I force myself to do more” but “is every action good, in the right direction, and do I repeat it”. If yes, even a little will be enough. If not, even an ocean won't help.
E
Environment
c (context) × ch (choice of surroundings)

And here's the letter everyone has the most complicated relationship with. Because it seems that circumstances are “fate”, “karma”, “who you're born to”, “where I live”. Things I don't influence. And that's a half-truth. Which is exactly why this letter is so tricky.

E is where you are right now in the broad sense. Country, city, family, the people around you, the cultural context, the historical moment. It's the field you play on. And yes, part of that field is already given. You were born where you were born. No one chooses the year they're born.

But inside E there are two parts, and they play differently.

c · context

What's given without your participation. The era, the language, the people around you in your first 18 years. This really can't be changed. And it has a strong influence; let's not fool ourselves or each other.

ch · choice of surroundings

And this can change. Who you let into your life now. Which city you live in. Who you spend your five closest hours a week with. Which books, podcasts and voices you let into your ears. Which professional circle you're part of.

Now the most important part. Imagine yourself in two different teams. In one job you're like a shadow, a “gray mouse”: you don't voice your ideas, you stay silent in meetings, you go home in the evening drained and not yourself. You move to another team, and within a few weeks you're unrecognizable. You laugh, suggest, argue, people hear you. You're still the same person. The field changed, and it brought out what the first environment was muffling.

The biggest influence in the formula. The least control. That's where almost all the surprises come from.

There's something here people rarely talk about, because it sounds a bit eerie. Neuroscience has a phenomenon called mirror neurons. They activate in your brain when you merely watch another person's action. As if you were doing it yourself. It's a learning mechanism, and at the same time a “contagion” mechanism. Spend time with people who constantly complain, and the same pattern starts up inside you. Spend time with people who get up and do things, and something in you starts moving too. Without any willpower on your part.

Sociologist Nicholas Christakis showed that happiness, depression, obesity and success all spread through social networks like a virus. Up to two steps away. Meaning you're influenced not only by your friend, but by your friend's friend, whom you never even meet. Through the friend you share.

An audit of your surroundings
Write down the five people you've spent the most time with in the past month (not by preference, by actual hours). Now be honest: where are they heading in life? Growing, standing still, falling? What values, what topics, what level of ambition? There's a well-known saying that we become like the five people we spend the most time with. It's more of a sharp observation than an exact scientific law, but it points in the right direction. Look at your picture. If you don't like it, change your surroundings. Don't try to remake people. Move yourself.
E in short: Don't blame yourself for being born somewhere other than where you'd have liked. Look at what you choose around you now: your city, your work, your people, the voices in your ears. Often it isn't about you, but about the field you're standing on. Moving yourself to another field is sometimes the strongest action of all.
O
Opportunities
n (noticing) × u (using)

Imagine: two people walk into the same café. One walks out with a new job. The other, with a coffee. From the outside, they were no different. What happened?

The first one saw an opportunity, the second didn't. Even though the opportunity was right there on the table for both.

O in the formula is about exactly this. And again, inside O two parts multiply.

n · noticing

The ability to see: this is it. A chance conversation in a line isn't just a conversation, it's a potential connection. A lecture on YouTube isn't “I should watch that someday”, it's a direct answer to your question right now. It's a skill. It can be trained.

u · using

The ability to take it. Noticing isn't enough; you still have to take a step. Walk up, write, try, ask. What blocks you here is fear of rejection, awkwardness, “not my business”. You can see twenty opportunities a day and use none of them.

Mushrooms grow in the forest. One person walks through and fills a basket. Another walks right beside them and sees nothing. Same mushrooms. The one who gathers them is the one who knows what they want to see, and who, having seen it, bends down to cut it. Noticing × using.

Opportunities aren't created. They're noticed and used.

In the brain, this is the job of selective attention. It's a filter that decides which of the millions of signals around you reach your awareness and which get discarded as noise. You look at a person in a new sweater, you notice the sweater, and your brain throws out the rest of the scene. That's not a mistake. It's a necessity: if everything your eyes see reached you, the flood would be unbearable.

But this filter learns. It lets through what you tune it to. If you've been actively thinking “where can I find a job” for the past few weeks, your brain suddenly starts hearing about jobs everywhere. A neighbor's conversation, a notice in a café, a Facebook post that a month ago would have gone unnoticed. The opportunities didn't appear. They were always there. They just used to go unnoticed.

Tuning the filter
For one week: write down one specific phrase, “I'm looking for X”. “I'm looking for a client for writing work.” “I'm looking for someone I can talk about my book with.” Reread it in the morning. In the evening, note what related to it flashed before your eyes that day. Don't expect it to happen right away. Just notice. After 7 days, look at your list. You won't believe how much is there.
O in short: There are always more opportunities than you see. The problem isn't that they're missing, but that your inner filter isn't tuned to them. Tune it, and they'll start “appearing”. Though really, they were always there; you just weren't noticing them.
Res
Resources
in (internal) ; ex (external) · change over time

Many people think resources mean money. That's true, but not the whole truth. If it were only about money, we wouldn't see millionaires lying burned out while people on small budgets build beautiful lives.

In the formula, Res is the fuel your engine runs on. And it splits into two circuits.

in · internal resources

What's inside you. There are five:

Abilities. What your brain and body already know how to do. Not “God-given talent” but accumulated neural architecture. What you repeat, what you pay attention to, what you invest time in.

Energy. Not figuratively. Literally: your body's ability to produce action without exhaustion. Sleep, food, movement, recovery. Without this, the whole formula stalls.

Attention. This is a separate currency. You can have plenty of energy and your attention scattered across twenty tasks, and nothing comes together.

Time. The time you actually control. Not “I have 24 hours”, but “I have two free hours after the kids are in bed”.

Psychological capital. Resilience, the ability to live through failures and come back. The ability not to get discouraged after the first try. This is a resource too, and it also gets spent and built up.

ex · external resources

Finances, social capital (who knows you and will put in a good word for you), tools (from a computer to internet access), information (access to what's going on and which rules your field plays by). These matter too. But I deliberately put them second.

Because there's a secret.

Resources aren't static. They change depending on your result. It's a loop. And it's the formula's greatest magic.

Look at the formula again. See the line “Res(t+1) = Res(t) + β · r(t)”? It means that what you do today is added to your resources for tomorrow. It doesn't reset you to zero, it adds.

Today you have a hard conversation, and tomorrow your psychological capital is a little bigger. Today you solve a new, difficult problem, and tomorrow your ability is a little greater. Today you meet someone, and tomorrow your social capital is bigger. Every action feeds the next one.

That's exactly why, ten years later, people who kept acting end up in completely different places from those who stood still. Not because they had a better start. But because their resources kept accumulating.

A resource inventory
Take an honest look at all five internal + four external resources. Not “rate from 1 to 10”, but write: where am I now. “Abilities: good at writing, average at negotiating, bad at bookkeeping.” “Energy: constantly drained in the mornings.” Find your weakest link. Not the worst in general, but the one that limits your formula right now. Work on that one, not all of them at once.
Res in short: Not “where do I get the resources to start”, but “what do I start so that resources begin to accumulate”. It's an inversion that sets you free. You don't need to wait until you're perfectly ready. You need to start the loop, and it will keep feeding you.
β
The conversion coefficient
how much of your result comes back into you as a resource

If anyone ever asks me which one component of the formula shapes your life ten years from now the most, I'll say: β.

Because this is what I once saw as a mystery. Why two people, from the same start, through the same events, the same failures and successes, end up in completely different places. Not “slightly different”: a chasm grows between them.

Two women open the same kind of business, and both fail within a year. One spends the next five years trying to recover; the other launches something new a year later, and it takes off. What's the difference? β.

β is how well you can learn from your own action. From your own result. From what you live through.

If β is high, every action, even a failed one, adds to your resources. Knowledge, experience, people, self-understanding. Tomorrow you start from a slightly better point.

If β is low, you can do a lot, go through a lot, fail a lot, and nothing accumulates. In the morning you're back at zero.

Think of compound interest in a bank. Put money in at 1% a year, and in 20 years you'll have a little more. At 7%, in 20 years you'll have about 4 times as much. The difference in the starting rate is tiny. The difference in the final sum is dramatic. That's because interest is earned on interest. Money grows on money.

β in the formula does the same with your resources. Each action brings a result. Part of the result comes back into you. Tomorrow's action starts with more resources. It brings a bigger result. More comes back. And so on.

What makes life exponential isn't persistence. It's β. The ability to learn from your own experience.

In the brain, β depends above all on the prefrontal cortex, your “command center”. In particular, on what neuroscientists call metacognition: thinking about your own thinking, watching how you act, and drawing conclusions from it.

It's a skill. It can be trained. And it's the best investment of your time there is. Because β multiplies the whole rest of the formula. Raise β by 10%, and everything accumulated over 10 years grows about five times.

What lowers β

The worst thing for β is avoiding what hurts. A failure, and you rush to forget it like a bad dream. Without looking at what actually happened. Then next time you'll step on the same rake, because your brain never recorded where it was lying.

The second is taking everything on yourself in defeat, and nothing in success. “It's my fault it didn't work” gets written into your identity (B). “I just got lucky” gets written nowhere. Ten years later, inside you there's only a list of failures, as if there were no successes. Though there were.

What raises β

An after-action journal. After every significant action (a good project, a failure, a hard conversation, a decision), three questions: What really happened? What did it teach me? What will I do differently next time?

Extracting the principle. Don't memorize the event. Extract a rule from it. “I'm bad at negotiating” isn't a rule. But “when I don't set my price in advance, I always agree to less” is a rule. That's something you can take into account and change next time.

Meta-attention. Ask not “what got done” but “what did this teach me”. A different focus, a different outcome.

The β ritual
Once a week, on a fixed day and time (for me it's Sunday evening), 15 minutes with a notebook. Three questions. What did this week teach me, and what important thing am I taking from it? What principle am I drawing from it? What am I taking forward to use in the future? If you do this for a year, in a year you'll literally become a different person. Not from “reading something”, but from extracting it from the week you lived.
β in short: This is the most influential point in the whole system, and almost nobody trains it consciously. It's what separates a person who lived ten years and grew from one who lived the same ten years and stayed in place. The key question here isn't “what's happening to me” but “how do I turn what's happening to me into experience”.
T
Time
the accumulation period · a nonlinear multiplier of everything

Time in the formula isn't just seconds and years. It's the period during which the loop gets to turn. And the longer it turns, the bigger the result. Not linearly. Exponentially.

Do you know the lovely bamboo allegory? They say some kinds of bamboo barely show above the ground for the first years after planting. From the outside it looks like nothing is growing, like the effort is wasted. And then, in a very short time, the shoot shoots up, as if by a miracle.

The secret is simple. While nothing is visible above, the bamboo is building its root: deep, branching, powerful. All the work goes down, into the invisible. And only when the root system is ready does the stem come up, at the speed the accumulated support allows.

A small difference at one step becomes a chasm over time. It's not a metaphor. It's math.

Imagine two people. One has β = 0.10. The other, β = 0.12. A trifle, right? Two percent, no miracle. Everything else is the same.

After a year, the second is a little ahead. Not very noticeable.

After five, they're clearly somewhere else.

After ten, it's a completely different life. No bragging, no aggressive moves, no excessive “willpower”. Just a better conversion of experience into resources. By two percent.

And this is the cruelest and the most beautiful part at once. The cruel part: you won't see the difference in the first year. It will seem like the changes lead nowhere. You'll want to quit. The beautiful part: if you don't quit, in five years things will be completely different. It's a question of trusting your own movement.

I made an interactive tool where you can play with the numbers and see for yourself how it works. It's right here further down the page, after all the letters.

A reminder for yourself
In the moments when it seems that “nothing is changing”, remember the bamboo. For most of any story of change, nothing is visible: the growth goes into the root. The visible part begins when the main thing has already happened inside. The most pointless thing is to quit right before the leap, because from the outside it looks no different from standing still.
T in short: Time isn't a passive background. It's an active multiplier of everything. And it doesn't reward those who run fast. It rewards those who use it wisely, step by step, without quitting halfway.
ε
Chance
noise · uncontrollable · ±

Honestly, there's a part of the result you don't control. And in this formula we honestly call it by its name. ε is noise. Good luck and bad luck. Something that adds or takes away regardless of what you do.

A meeting that changed everything because you happened to be running late and didn't go to that café on another day. A plane that left without you. A person who noticed you. Or the opposite: an illness that wrecked your plans. A bombing that destroyed a city. I'm writing from Ukraine, and I know very well how ε can wipe out a large part of the formula overnight.

Build resilience, not an illusion of control. Account for ε on both sides.

There's a paradox here. The better trained the rest of your formula is, the more you get from a positive ε. Luck comes to everyone; some are ready to take it, and others walk right past.

And the better you build the β and Res loop, the better you withstand a negative ε. Because you have something to start over with. Accumulated abilities, accumulated psychological capital, accumulated people around you.

So the right strategy toward chance isn't trying to control it (pointless), but building a formula that handles both the plus and the minus well.

ε in short: Not “nothing depends on me”. And not “everything depends on me”. But honestly: a lot depends on me, some things don't. The first is your job. The second is your resilience.
Π
Rules of the game
laws of nature · social norms · the rules of the field you play on

And this is what stands above the whole formula. Not a variable inside it, but the frame within which the formula can exist at all.

I mark it with the Greek letter Pi, Π. Let's agree right away so we don't mix things up: B, Beliefs, is a filter inside you. Π, the rules of the game, is a frame outside you, the field you play on. Keep that difference in mind until the end of this section.

Π is the rules of the game. There are three layers here.

Laws of nature

What's set by physics, biology, time and space. A day has 24 hours, not 30. The body needs sleep. Broken connections hurt. It's not “unfair”, it's how things are. Trying to get around it eats resources and produces no result.

Social norms

The rules of the society you live in. What's valued and what isn't. Which professions, which style. What's allowed for a woman and what for a man. Different in different times and countries. These are flexible rules: you can break them, but at a price. You pay with attention, gossip, relationships. Sometimes it's worth paying. Sometimes not. That's your decision.

The rules of your particular field

The most flexible layer. The rules of your industry, your niche, your city, your specific situation. What paths exist here, what barriers, how people grow here. IT has one set of rules, art another, science a third.

The shortest way to explain Π: imagine chess and checkers. On the same 64-square board. If you sit down to play checkers while your partner plays chess, you'll keep losing without understanding why. Your rules don't apply. Your whole strategy is useless not because it's bad, but because it's the wrong game.

Π sets the range of possible results. Know the rules, and you win even with weaker components. Don't know them, and you lose even with the best.

That's why moving from one country to another sometimes changes everything. Not because the person became someone else, but because the rules are different there. What was punished here is rewarded there. What was considered “a dead end” here is in demand there.

An inventory of the rules of your game
Write down which game you're playing right now. Not “I have a career in X”, but “the rules of this particular game are such-and-such”. What's valued? What's punished? Which paths are open, which aren't? Who are the referees, who are the players? Often the problem isn't you, but that you're playing the wrong game. Or playing the right one, but not by its rules.
Π in short: Before you play, make sure you understand the game. Otherwise all your effort goes into flawlessly performing the wrong moves.
The summary frame

Three iron laws there's no way out of

Three laws follow from the formula. Not “principles”, not “guidelines”, but laws. Like gravity: you may not believe in them, but they work without your consent.

Law I
The law of zero
Any component = 0 → the whole result = 0. Find your zero and work on it, instead of fixing everything at once.
Law II
The law of sabotage
If B goes negative, the formula works against you. The more effort, the worse it gets. The pressure point here is identity, not discipline.
Law III
The law of the chasm
A small difference at one step → becomes a chasm over time. It's exponential. You won't see it in a week. You see it over a long enough stretch of time.

One last thing before we go to the lab: these laws aren't a verdict. They're a map. If it seems to you that “every door is closed”, look closely at which law it belongs to. Almost always it's Law I (a zero somewhere) or Law II (sabotage somewhere).

The exercise map: what grows from where

If the formula is honest, it should tell you what to train. Every exercise in the workbooks grows from a specific part of the equation. Not “personal development in general”, but targeted work on a specific component. Here are five directions, each tied directly to the formula.

βloop
Converting results into resources
The most influential point. The higher β, the faster the exponential.
An after-action journal. After every significant event, three questions: what happened, what I understand from it, what will change in me.
Extracting the principle. Every week, one rule drawn from your own experience.
Meta-attention. The question isn't “what happened” but “how did it change me”.
A·Enovelty
Building a new neural network
The network grows more complex through what the brain has never done before. Repetition myelinates the old. Novelty builds new bridges.
One new experience a week. A fundamentally new kind of activity.
Changing sensory patterns. The other hand, a different route, a different context for a familiar task.
Crossing disciplines. Physical-mental hybrids.
LTPconsolidation
Intensity × multiple channels
The strength of new neural connections = vividness × number of channels × emotional marker. Weak, monotonous experience means weak connections.
Multisensory practice. Sight + hearing + touch + movement at once.
An emotional marker before you start. A clear “this matters to me” before the practice.
Peak instead of duration. 20 vivid minutes beat 2 boring hours.
B(i·c)filter
Beliefs and identity
B is the only component that can make R negative. The work here is spotting sabotage patterns and rebuilding identity.
An identity audit. 20 endings to the sentence “I am a person who…”.
Calibrating with facts. Testing limiting beliefs against evidence.
Action that runs ahead of belief. A small action from your new identity.
Res(in)fuel
Energy, attention, time
An engine without fuel doesn't run. Res(in) isn't “a self-development luxury”, it's the basic condition for the formula to work.
Sleep and recovery hygiene. Not “when I get around to it”, but as a required part of the system.
Focus training. Disconnecting from information noise.
Spotting windows of O. The metacognitive skill of noticing opportunities.
What to do with this

The formula isn't a verdict. It's a map.

Now that you see the whole map, you have a rare advantage: you don't have to fix everything at once. Not “become the best version of yourself”. Not “level up your life”.

Instead, take an honest look and find your pressure point. For some it's D (moving in the wrong direction). For some, B (sabotage from within). For some, E (surroundings that drain you). For some, β (living a lot, but not learning).

Usually it's one or two components. The rest are fine. And changing one of them changes everything, because the formula multiplies.

Remember the girl from the beginning who thought she was unlucky? What she spent years searching for is now in front of you, all of it, on one page. Back then I saw only ε, a random lottery ticket, and thought it decided everything. But the rest of the formula was in my hands the whole time. Luck really can give you a good start. But it won't build the big Result for you. You build it yourself, from small daily steps.
