β

How to learn to grow from your own experience

Ten exercises on the most powerful component of the Results Formula

Remember the β component from the lecture, the one I called the heart of the formula? It's the ability to learn from your own experience. Not from books, not from other people's advice, but from what you live through yourself.

Why does the workbook start with it? Because β multiplies everything. You can train your actions, look for opportunities, protect your resources, but if you can't turn what you've lived through into conclusions, every day starts from zero. With a good β, even a failure becomes a step up.

And here's the most important thing to hear right away: β isn't a character trait you're born with. It's a skill. You can train it just like a muscle. That's exactly what we're going to do.

Some people live ten years and grow. Others live the same ten years and stay where they were. The difference is β.

Below are ten exercises, arranged in sequence. They take you through three levels: first noticing your experience, then extracting conclusions from it, and finally applying them in your next step. That's how the loop closes, and that loop is where growth comes from.

Before you begin

How to use this workbook

1
This isn't reading, it's work. The exercises are meant to be done, not skimmed. A workbook you've read changes nothing. A workbook you've done changes a lot.
2
In order. The exercises follow a logical sequence. The first level is the foundation for the second, the second for the third. Don't skip ahead.
3
Don't rush. This isn't a one-day marathon. Take one exercise, live with it for a few days until it becomes familiar, and only then move on.
4
Tick an exercise when it has become yours. Not when you've read it, but when you actually do it without effort. The progress bar at the top shows how much of the path is behind you.
5
Don't fool yourself. There's no one here to show your results to. All the value lies in being honest with yourself.
Level 1 · Foundation
Notice

Before you can learn from experience, you have to learn to see it at all. We live most of the day on autopilot, and then we're surprised that nothing changes.

Exercise 1
The after-action journal
mechanism: metacognition · prefrontal cortex pain: "living on autopilot"

The simplest and most important exercise in the whole workbook. The idea is to stop for a few minutes every day and look at what you've lived through from the outside. It's the basic training ground for β: you teach your brain not just to live through events, but to notice them.

How to do it
  1. Pick a fixed time, ideally in the evening before bed. Tie it to something you already do every day (after dinner, after your shower).
  2. Ask yourself three questions and answer briefly, out loud or in your notes: What important thing happened today? What do I feel about it? What do I notice about myself?
  3. Keep it short. Two or three sentences. The goal isn't "keeping a diary", it's training your attention to stop.
Example: "At today's meeting I kept quiet, even though I had something to say. I feel annoyed with myself. I notice I go quiet every time my manager is in the room."
Checkpoint: the exercise is working when you catch yourself observing in the middle of the day: "oh, that's worth writing down". It means your attention has learned to notice.
Exercise 2
The weekly β ritual
mechanism: memory consolidation · reflection pain: "I'm capable of more, but I'm standing still"

If the after-action journal is daily hygiene, this ritual is your weekly review. A week is long enough to see a pattern, not just a single event.

How to do it
  1. Choose one day and time each week, and keep it fixed. Mine is Sunday evening. Fifteen minutes, no more.
  2. Reread your daily notes for the week (if you have them) and answer three questions.
  3. What did I learn this week, and what's important for me to take from it? What principle do I draw from it? What do I take forward to apply in the future?
Example: "This week I twice agreed to things I didn't want. Conclusion: I say 'yes' automatically when I'm caught off guard. What I take forward: in moments like that, say 'let me think and get back to you'."
Checkpoint: you haven't skipped the ritual three weeks in a row, and it no longer feels like a duty. You start to feel that the week has been "completed", not just ended.
Exercise 3
Catch the autopilot
mechanism: awareness of habitual patterns · basal ganglia pain: "I don't know what I really want"

A large part of life runs on autopilot, and that's not a flaw, it's the brain's way of saving energy. But it's right there, on autopilot, that the reactions you're not happy with are hiding. This exercise teaches you to catch them.

How to do it
  1. During the day, catch the moments when you do something without thinking: a habitual reaction, an automatic "yes", the usual route, the usual irritation.
  2. In that moment, don't scold yourself. Just make a mental note: "that was autopilot".
  3. In the evening, write down one or two of these moments. You don't need to change anything yet. First, just see.
Example: "Again I open my phone the moment I'm alone, without even deciding to. Autopilot. I notice I do it every time I start feeling anxious."
Checkpoint: you start catching the autopilot in the moment, not only when looking back in the evening. That's the first crack in the habit. Next comes choice.
Level 2 · Core
Extract

Noticing isn't enough. Now for the most important part: turning what you've seen into conclusions. An event into a principle you can apply again.

Exercise 4
Extracting the principle
mechanism: generalisation · abstracting from experience pain: "whatever I do, it never feels like enough"

This is the core of β. An event teaches nothing by itself. What teaches is the principle you draw from it. Because a principle can be applied again, and an event can't: it's already over.

How to do it
  1. Take any significant event from the week, good or bad.
  2. Ask yourself: "What does this say about how I usually act?" Look not for a judgement of the event, but for a pattern.
  3. Phrase it as a rule: briefly, in your own words, in the form "when…, I…".
Example: "I'm bad at negotiating" isn't a principle, it's a verdict. But "when I don't decide my price in advance, I always agree to less" is a principle. You can take it into account next time.
Checkpoint: you have a personal list of principles, and you turn to it from time to time. It's your own book of wisdom, drawn from your own life, not someone else's.
Exercise 5
Unpacking a failure without beating yourself up
mechanism: separating fact from self-worth · working with the amygdala pain: "I'm always waiting for something to go wrong"

β suffers most when a failure turns into self-blame. If a setback becomes "I'm a failure", the brain wants to forget it as fast as possible, and the lesson never gets recorded. The rake stays right where it was, waiting for you to step on it again. This exercise teaches you to unpack a failure calmly.

How to do it
  1. Name what happened as a fact, without judging yourself. Not "I ruin everything", but "the project was handed in a week late".
  2. Separate the action from the person: it's not about you, a specific step didn't work.
  3. Ask: "What exactly will I do differently next time?" One specific change, not ten.
Example: Instead of "nothing ever works out for me, it all went wrong again" → "I take on more than I can manage. Next time I'll state the real deadlines right away, not the ones people want to hear".
Checkpoint: after a failure you feel not only pain, but curiosity too: "what can I learn here?". It means β has started digesting setbacks into experience, not into guilt.
Exercise 6
Own your success
mechanism: attribution · reinforcing positive experience pain: "I need to be praised, recognised"

Strangely, we unpack success even less often than failure. "I just got lucky", "anyone could have done it", and the lesson never gets recorded. Years later, what's left inside is a long list of failures and not a single success, although there were some. This exercise fixes that.

How to do it
  1. Recall something that went well this week, even something small.
  2. Ask honestly: "What was my part here, not luck?" Not "I got lucky", but your own specific contribution.
  3. Write it down as one of your strong moves. Something worth repeating on purpose.
Example: "The conversation with the client went well, and not by accident. I'd thought through my questions in advance and didn't interrupt. That worked. I'll keep doing it."
Checkpoint: you can name three of your strong moves that really work. Not abstract "virtues", but specific skills you notice in yourself and acknowledge.
Exercise 7
Calibrating your beliefs
mechanism: checking beliefs against facts · working with B(i) pain: "my mood and self-esteem depend on other people"

Here β meets the B component: beliefs. We often carry "that's just the kind of person I am" as an unquestionable truth, even though the facts say otherwise. This exercise checks an inner belief against reality.

How to do it
  1. Write down one limiting belief about yourself. "I can't speak in public", "nobody listens to me", "I always put things off".
  2. Now honestly look for facts that contradict it. When was it different? Even just once.
  3. Rephrase the belief so it has room for the facts too. Not "I can't", but "it's sometimes hard for me, but I've done it before".
Example: "Nobody listens to me" → I remember: at the last meeting, people did listen to my suggestion. So the truth is more like: "I'm not heard when I speak unsure of myself. When I speak with confidence, I'm heard".
Checkpoint: you catch yourself in the old belief and immediately recall a fact that contradicts it. The rigid "that's just the kind of person I am" starts to soften into "sometimes it's like that, sometimes it isn't".
Level 3 · Consolidation
Apply

The last and decisive level. A conclusion that goes nowhere is dead. Here we close the loop: we carry what we've extracted into the next step.

Exercise 8
Preloading the next step
mechanism: closing the loop · implementation intention pain: "living on autopilot"

This is the bridge from understanding to action. A conclusion by itself doesn't change behaviour. What changes it is an intention, set in advance, to apply that conclusion in a specific situation.

How to do it
  1. Take one principle you've already extracted (from exercise 4).
  2. Imagine a specific situation in the next few days where you'll need it.
  3. Phrase it in advance: "When X happens, I'll do Y". Clearly, like an agreement with yourself.
Example: Principle: "I say 'yes' automatically when I'm caught off guard". Preloading: "When I'm asked on Friday to take on another task, I'll say 'let me think about it until tomorrow', not an instant 'yes'".
Checkpoint: at least once, in a real situation, you acted on the intention you'd set in advance, not on the old autopilot. That's the moment the β loop closed for the first time.
Exercise 9
A map of the rakes you keep stepping on
mechanism: spotting patterns · long-term memory pain: "I'm capable of more, but I'm standing still"

Once you've collected a few weeks of observations, an unpleasant but very useful picture emerges: you keep stepping on the same rakes. The same situations, the same reactions. Seeing them together is half the job.

How to do it
  1. Look through your notes from the last month and search for repeats. What comes up more than once?
  2. Write down 2–3 of your most frequent "rakes": recurring situations with the same unwanted ending.
  3. Next to each, note the rescue move: what you already know how to do differently (from the previous exercises).
Example: "The rake: I take on too much because I'm afraid to say no → I get exhausted → I snap at the people close to me. The rescue move: answer 'I'll think about it' and check any new load against what I already have."
Checkpoint: you recognise your rakes while you're still approaching them ("oh, this is that same situation again") and manage to turn aside. Before, you only saw it after the fact.
Exercise 10
The monthly β review
mechanism: meta-reflection · assessing your own growth pain: "I don't know what I really want"

The highest level is observing not events, but how you yourself are changing. Once a month, you rise above the weeks and look at your trajectory as a whole. It's β turned on β itself.

How to do it
  1. Once a month, at a quiet time, set aside half an hour. Reread your weekly reviews.
  2. Answer: "What has changed in me this month? What do I now understand about myself that was invisible a month ago?"
  3. Give yourself one focus for the next month: one thing you'll work on consciously.
Example: "In a month, I've started noticing when I say 'yes' out of fear. I don't always stop yet, but I can see it now. My focus for next month: say an honest 'no' at least three times."
Checkpoint: you can say in two sentences how you differ from who you were a month ago. If you can, β is working. You are literally growing from your own experience.
How to tell your β is growing

Signs, not numbers

You can't measure β in points. But there are clear signs that it's getting stronger. Check in with them once a month.

You catch your reactions in the moment more and more often, not only when looking back in the evening.
After a failure there's not only pain, but curiosity too: "what can I learn here?".
You have your own list of principles, drawn from life, and you use it.
You recognise your "rakes" on the approach and manage to turn aside.
You can name how you differ from yourself a month ago.
Careful

What lowers β

Three things quietly undo all the work. Know them by sight.

Avoiding what hurts. "Let's just forget it, like a bad dream." Then the lesson never gets recorded, and you'll step on the same rake again.
Taking all the blame for defeat and no credit for success. "It's my fault" gets recorded, "I got lucky there" doesn't. Years later, all that's left inside is a list of failures.
Confusing reflection with self-flagellation. Unpacking experience is a calm look from the outside. If the exercises become an excuse to beat yourself up, stop and go back to exercise 5.
To finish

Questions worth sitting with

1. What experience from the past year have I still not allowed myself to make sense of, because it hurt too much?
2. Which "rakes" have been with me for many years? What do I never take away from them?
3. Which of my successes do I never claim as my own, putting it all down to luck or other people?
4. If I turned every week I live through into a conclusion, how different would my year be?
5. What's stopping me from starting today?
And finally

Remember the main thing: you don't have to do all ten exercises in a week. It's enough to start with the first one, the after-action journal, and let it become a habit. One exercise lived well changes more than ten you've read.

β is the percentage by which you grow from each day you live. It seems like a trifle. But over time, it's exactly what turns two almost identical trajectories into two completely different lives.

What small step will you take today?

Svitlana Slipchenko, psychologist, author of Beyond the Horizon
