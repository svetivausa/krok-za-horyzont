How to start wanting your own things again

Ten exercises on the component that starts the whole results formula

In the results formula, D, Desire, is the engine. The key in the ignition. However good the car, however good the fuel and smooth the road, nothing moves until the key is turned. Desire switches everything else on.

You'd think there's nothing to practise here: who doesn't want things? But here's the trap. Most of "I can't make myself", "no motivation", "I know I should, but I don't" is actually not about willpower. It's about a desire that is either not real, or not yours, or went out long ago without you noticing.

Neuroscience has a discovery that explains all of this. Back in the nineties, Kent Berridge showed that "wanting" and "liking" are two different signals in the brain. Wanting runs on dopamine and pulls you towards action. Liking is a different system, the one about pleasure. And they can come apart.

That's where the strangest part comes from. You can want something without getting any joy from it. Scrolling the feed for hours: your hand reaches for it, yet it pleases you less and less. Or the opposite: you may feel no pull towards what truly matters to you, because years of "should" have muffled the living signal of wanting.

Real desire is when wanting and liking meet. Everything else is either a habit or someone else's voice.

This workbook takes you through three levels: first hearing your own desire among the imposed and the habitual, then checking whether it's really alive, and finally kindling it so it moves you instead of staying a dream in your head.

Before you begin
If during one of the exercises you suddenly find that you haven't wanted almost anything for a long time, or the question "what do I want" makes you anxious or empty, that's no reason to panic and no sign that something is wrong with you. Faded desire is a common companion of exhaustion and burnout, and that's a separate, important topic. Be gentle with yourself. If you feel there's more here than simple uncertainty, it's worth reaching out for support to people close to you or to a professional.
How it works

How to use this workbook

1
This is work, not reading. Desire doesn't come back from thinking about it. It comes back from honest questions and small actions.
2

3

Level 1 · Foundation
Hear your own

Before kindling a desire, you need to make sure it's yours. Because most of our "I want" is actually either someone else's voice or an old habit pretending to be desire.

Exercise 1
The authenticity test
mechanism: separating intrinsic from extrinsic motivation pain: "I live on autopilot, I don't know what I want"

The quickest way to check whether a desire is yours is to remove the audience. Because a lot of "I want" is really about proving something, pleasing someone, not falling behind. Remove the witnesses, and you'll see what is left that's truly yours.

How to do it
  1. Take a desire you consider your own. "I want to lose weight", "I want my own business", "I want to learn Spanish".
  2. Ask yourself honestly: "would I still want this if nobody ever found out about the result? No social media, no mum, no old classmates?"
  3. Listen to your body's first reaction. If inside it's "yes, I'd still want it", the desire is real. If it's "then what's the point?", it wasn't yours.
Example. "I want to learn Spanish", and what if nobody found out? Suddenly it's clear: what I really wanted wasn't the language, but not to feel worse than the colleagues who travel abroad. The pressure goes, and so does the "desire". So it wasn't mine.
Checkpoint: you can name at least one desire that survived this test, and one that fell apart. Just telling them apart frees up a lot of energy that was going into someone else's wants.
Exercise 2
Want, or just used to it
mechanism: the dopamine loop of habit versus living desire pain: "tired of 'should', life on autopilot"

Dopamine is a tricky thing. It keeps you in the loop even when the pleasure is long gone: the feed, sugar, late-night series. Your hand reaches out, and the joy is zero. That's wanting without liking. This exercise teaches you to catch the difference.

How to do it
  1. Think of something you do "on autopilot" every day, as if you wanted it. Your phone in the morning, something sweet in the evening, endless scrolling.
  2. Next time, while you're doing it, ask yourself: "is this actually pleasant right now, or can I just not stop?"
  3. Write the answer down honestly. No judgement. Just note where the wanting is alive and where only bare habit is left.
Example. "Every evening I open the feed. I think I'm resting. But when I look closer: the pleasure is gone by the third minute, and my finger keeps scrolling. That's not a wish to rest. That's a loop."
Checkpoint: you catch at least one of your "loops" while it's spinning, and for the first time you see it isn't real wanting, just inertia. What you can see is easier to stop.
Exercise 3
Whose "should" is it
mechanism: attributing the source of motivation pain: "I always owe everyone, where am I in all this"

Many of our "goals" are other people's voices we've taken for our own. Your mum wanted you to have a reliable profession. Society says that by thirty you should have this and that. These "shoulds" sound so natural they seem to be ours. This exercise returns them to their owners.

How to do it
  1. Write down 5–7 of your "I have to / I should". Things that press with a sense of duty rather than pull with desire.
  2. Next to each one, ask: "whose voice is this, really? Who was the first to tell me it has to be this way?"
  3. Sort them: this one is truly mine and I sign up for it myself; and this one is someone else's voice that I can quietly hand back: "thank you, but this is your 'should', not mine".
Example. "I have to build a career in law": whose voice? My dad's. He dreamed of it for himself. And I don't care about law. I can respectfully leave him his dream, and go looking for my own.
Checkpoint: your list of "shoulds" has noticeably fewer items. What's left feels lighter, because it really is yours, not borrowed.
Level 2 · Core
Check the fire

Telling your own from someone else's isn't enough. Now the main thing: is this desire really alive, or just an idea in your head? A living pull has signs you can recognise it by.

Exercise 4
"Want" versus "like"
mechanism: Berridge's distinction between wanting and liking pain: "whatever I do, it never feels like enough"

This is the heart of the whole workbook. Remember the two signals: wanting (it pulls) and liking (it pleases)? Real desire, the kind that gives you strength, is when they meet. When something pulls but doesn't please, emptiness lies ahead. This exercise teaches you to check both.

How to do it
  1. Take a desire that passed the first level. Break it into two questions.
  2. Do I want it? Does it pull me, does the thought of it give me energy?
  3. Do I like it? When I'm actually doing it (or imagine the process in detail, not just the result), do I really feel good? Or is it only "I have to get through this"?
Example. "I want to start a blog." Does it pull? Yes, the thought is exciting. Do I like the process itself: writing, filming, editing? …And here comes a pause. It turns out I want the fame, not the work. The two signals came apart, and it's honest to see it before, not after a year of struggle.
Checkpoint: for any desire you can ask separately "does it pull?" and "does it please?", and you notice that the most alive desires are the ones where both signals say yes.
Exercise 5
The body compass
mechanism: interoception · body signals of desire pain: "I don't know what I really want"

The head can lie about desire: it knows what you're "supposed" to want. The body can't. A living desire almost always has a bodily response: warmth, expansion, lightness in the chest. An imposed one, the opposite: tightness, heaviness, fatigue. The body is a more accurate compass than thoughts.

How to do it
  1. Sit comfortably and close your eyes. One by one, imagine a few of your desires, in detail, as if they had already come true.
  2. After each one, listen to your body. Where is the response? Does your chest open or tighten? Does breathing get easier or harder?
  3. Give each desire a body mark: "warm / light" or "tight / heavy". Your body will show you what's truly yours.
Example. I imagine "a big position": my chest tightens, my shoulders sink, as if a weight has already been dropped on them. I imagine "a small workshop where I make things with my hands", and suddenly it's warm and easy to breathe. The body said what the head was too shy to admit.
Checkpoint: you start noticing the body's response to desire not only in the exercise but in life, and you trust it as a hint when your head gets tangled.
Exercise 6
What you wanted before all the "shoulds"
mechanism: access to early, uncensored desires pain: "I've lost myself, I'm living someone else's life"

Before "should", "shameful" and "not serious" appeared, everyone had pure desires. Not childish in the sense of naive, but not yet censored by adult rules. Very often that's exactly where the hint about what you really want is waiting. This isn't about going back to the past, it's about access to a signal that hasn't been muffled.

How to do it
  1. Remember a specific moment before the age of ten or twelve when you felt truly good. What were you doing? Not "what you wanted to become", but what absorbed you in the process.
  2. Write down a few of those activities. Drawing, taking gadgets apart, making up stories, leading the games in the yard, looking after animals.
  3. Now look not at the activities themselves, but at what pleased you in them. Creating? Figuring out how things work? Being among people? Those are your lasting desires, and they haven't gone anywhere.
Example. "As a child I spent hours running a 'hospital' for my toys." It's not about the hospital. What pleased me was caring, fixing, making things whole again. And today I feel most alive where I can look after someone. The signal hasn't changed in thirty years.
Checkpoint: you see the thread running from a childhood passion to what brings you to life now. Desire stops seeming "lost": it was just under a layer of "shoulds".
Exercise 7
Anti-desires
mechanism: defining desires through negation pain: "I can't set boundaries or say no"

Sometimes the question "what do I want" is so big that the only answer is emptiness. Then you come at it from the other side: what do I definitely not want. A person's "no" is often clearer than their "yes", and it also shows a direction. Boundaries are a form of desire too, just turned inside out.

How to do it
  1. Without overthinking, write down 10 things you definitely don't want in your life. Ways of living, activities, states, certain kinds of people.
  2. Write sharply and honestly: this is only for you. "I don't want to wake up at six." "I don't want to keep pleasing people." "I don't want a job where everyone is grey."
  3. Now turn each "I don't want" around and see which "I want" is hiding behind it. Desire is often easier to see from the shadow of its opposite.
Example. "I don't want to keep explaining why I'm resting" → behind it lives the desire to allow myself calm without excuses. "I don't want to work alone in front of a screen" → I want living people around me. The anti-desire showed the direction.
Checkpoint: out of your "don't want" list a few clear "I want"s were born that you couldn't put into words before. Sometimes this is the easiest way back to yourself.
Level 3 · Consolidation
Kindle it and keep it fed

The most important level. A desire you've heard and checked still moves nothing while it stays a dream in your head. Here we turn it into a living pull, and learn not to let it go out.

Exercise 8
From "should" to "want"
mechanism: reframing motivation · intrinsic reward pain: "tired of 'should', everything takes force"

The same task can live in your head as a "should", and then it drains you, or as a "want", and then it gives you energy by itself. Often what needs changing isn't the task but the language you think about it in. Because "should" switches on resistance, and "want" switches on the engine.

How to do it
  1. Take a task you drag yourself through out of "should" (but one that really matters to you, not someone else's).
  2. Ask: "for the sake of what of mine am I doing this? What good does it give me or open up for me?" Look not for duty, but for your own benefit or value.
  3. Rewrite the sentence. Not "I have to sort out my finances", but "I want to feel calm and steady, so I'm putting my money in order".
Example. "I should exercise" drains me. I rewrite it: "I want to climb stairs easily and have energy for my kids in the evening." The same action, but now my own living desire stands behind it, not the voice of a doctor or of shame.
Checkpoint: you've rewritten at least one important task from "should" to "want", and felt your attitude to it change. Less resistance, more pull.
Exercise 9
The smallest spark
mechanism: dopamine from action, not from dreaming · behavioural activation pain: "I could do more, but I'm stuck in place"

A big trap of desire is waiting for it to become strong before you start. It works the other way round. Dopamine flares up from the first movement, not from the dream. The smallest real action towards a desire feeds it more than hours of imagining. The spark lights the fire, not the other way round.

How to do it
  1. Take a living desire that passed the check. Don't set a big goal: that puts it out.
  2. Come up with the smallest possible action towards it. So small you'll definitely do it today in 10 minutes. Not "learn the language", but "listen to one song in that language with the lyrics".
  3. Do it, and notice what happened to the desire after the action. Almost always it flares up rather than fades.
Example. The desire to draw had lain dead for years, because in my head it was "you have to study properly". The smallest spark: buy one pencil and sketch something on a napkin over coffee. And suddenly I want more. The fire caught from a spark, not from plans.
Checkpoint: you've seen it for yourself: action first, desire follows. And you no longer wait for "a big want" to take a small step.
Exercise 10
Keep the fire fed
mechanism: protecting motivation from routine · regular feeding pain: "unfulfilled, my desire fades in everyday life"

Most often desire doesn't die from a big defeat but quietly, smothered by daily life, tiredness, endless "later". A fire nobody feeds goes out. The final exercise is about feeding your desire consciously so it doesn't go out again.

How to do it
  1. Work out what feeds your particular desire: a small regular action, certain people, a place, music, the memory of "why".
  2. Work out what puts it out: exhaustion, other people's "that's not serious", comparing yourself with others, putting it off "until better times".
  3. Set yourself one simple feeding ritual: a weekly contact with your desire. Small, but regular. A fire doesn't love big logs once a year, it loves a little firewood often.
Example. What feeds it: an hour every Sunday "just for my own thing", without guilt. What puts it out: feeds of successful people that leave me deflated. Decision: Sunday is sacred, and comparison gets less attention. The desire got protection.
Checkpoint: you have a specific ritual that keeps the desire alive, and you know your "fire-killers" by sight. Your desire is no longer at the mercy of chance: you take care of it.
How to tell you're hearing your desire

Signs, not scores

Desire can't be measured in numbers. But there are signs that you're hearing it. Check in once a month.

For any "I want" you can ask separately: does it pull? and does it please?
You catch your dopamine loops (the feed, sugar) and tell them apart from living desire.
You trust the body's response, warmth or tightness, as a hint when your head gets tangled.
Your list of "shoulds" has fewer items: you've handed some back to their owners.
You no longer wait for "a big want": you know the spark comes from action.
Careful

What throws you off

Three traps that make it easy to confuse desire with something else.

Mistaking excitement for desire. The adrenaline of something new, the thrill, the anxious "everyone's running, so am I": that isn't always your "I want". Check it against liking and against your body.
Chasing someone else's. The worst thing is to reach your dream and feel empty. That's a sign the desire wasn't yours. Better to check before than after years.
Waiting for "a big want". Strong desire rarely comes first. More often it flares up along the way. Waiting for it before you start means never starting.
To finish

Questions worth sitting with

1. What would I want if absolutely nobody ever found out, neither the people close to me nor social media?
2. Which of my "goals" are actually other people's voices that I've taken for my own?
3. When did I last want something so much that it pulled and pleased me at the same time?
4. What is the smallest spark that could bring my desire to life today?
5. What in my life quietly puts out my desires, and can I weaken it?
And finally

Desire is the engine of the formula. While it's silent or not yours, you can have everything else, actions, resources, opportunities, and still not move. Because the key hasn't been turned.

You don't need to force yourself to want the "right" things. It's enough to give yourself permission to hear what you really want, even if it's quiet and modest. You'll recognise your own desire by the fact that it pulls and pleases at the same time.

What do you really want, once you remove all the "shoulds"?

Svitlana Slipchenko, psychologist, author of Beyond the Horizon
